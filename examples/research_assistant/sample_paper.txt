Effects of Structured Study Breaks on Delayed Recall

We investigated whether short, scheduled breaks improve delayed recall during
independent study. Sixty volunteer students aged 16 to 18 were randomly assigned
to one of two study schedules. The continuous-study group read a 2,400-word
history passage for 30 minutes. The structured-break group studied the same
passage for three 10-minute blocks separated by two-minute quiet breaks. Phones
and reference materials were unavailable during both conditions.

Participants completed a 20-question multiple-choice test immediately after
studying and again seven days later. The immediate scores were similar: the
continuous-study group averaged 15.1 correct answers and the structured-break
group averaged 15.4. At seven days, the continuous-study group averaged 11.2
correct answers, while the structured-break group averaged 13.6. The between-
group difference in delayed scores was 2.4 questions (95% confidence interval
1.1 to 3.7, p = 0.001).

The scheduled breaks were associated with better delayed recall without a clear
change in immediate performance. Because the study used one history passage,
the result may not generalize to mathematical problem solving or longer study
sessions. Volunteers came from one school, and neither participants nor test
administrators were blinded to the assigned schedule. The study recorded test
scores but did not measure attention directly, so it cannot determine why the
break schedule affected recall.
